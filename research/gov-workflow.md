# Government flood workflows in Cambodia: evidence base for MekongPulse

## 1. The reporting chain and its artifacts

Sub-decree 30 (2002) set up the national and sub-national committees, sub-decree 61 (2006) the Commune Committees for Disaster Management, and Direction 315 (2010) village teams, so the chain NCDM to PCDM to DCDM to CCDM is legally defined, not informal [S6].

The loss-and-damage artifact is defined and already partly digital. NCDM keeps a standard reporting form for loss and damage; the data are collected with KoBoToolbox on mobile and reported through WFP's Platform for Real-time Impact and Situation Monitoring (PRISM). CCDMs and DCDMs report the data, PCDMs pass them up, and NCDM approves them for official use. Fields cover affected population, affected and damaged houses, agriculture and infrastructure, and priority needs by sector (shelter, education, water-sanitation, health, food and cash, protection) [S1]. PRISM has run in Cambodia since 2015-2016, links IDPoor with hazard data, and supports export for further analysis [S1][S9]. CAMDI, NCDM's historical loss-and-damage database, was rebuilt in 2020 around Sendai indicators [S1][S14]. AHA Centre WebEOC is connected but only activated during a declared disaster [S1].

Transmission today, as documented: loss-and-damage figures move digitally through KoBoToolbox and PRISM between CCDM, DCDM, PCDM and NCDM [S1]; citizen-facing warnings move by IVR call, loudspeaker, radio and car-mounted speaker [S1][S3][S8]. No source reviewed documents paper loss-and-damage forms still in use, and no source documents a national flood coordination group on Telegram or Viber. Both are absences of evidence in the reviewed set, not proof.

SitReps are the public-facing artifact, and HRF writes them, not NCDM. SitRep No. 2 (1 Sep to 11 Oct 2022) reports 85,482 households affected in 14 provinces, 4,498 households evacuated, 15 deaths, 33,165 houses, 29 health centres, 280 schools, 1,607,260 m of rural road and 152,386 ha of agricultural land affected. It also states that responses were "manageable at provincial level" and that PCDMs and humanitarian actors delivered relief [S2]. That field list is what any commune-level summary should mirror.

For 2026 the same shape holds: NCDM reported 10 provinces, 53 districts, 255 communes and 73,922 households affected as of 29 September 2026 [S17].

Hydro-meteorological warnings flow separately and mostly by email, fax and phone. MOWRAM's Meteorological and Hydrology departments issue three-day and nowcast products to the Royal Palace, the Prime Minister's cabinet, NCDM, Ministry of Information, Cambodian Red Cross, Ministry of Interior and Ministry of National Defense, with sub-national delivery through provincial administrations and PCDMs plus media [S1, Figure 10]. Earlier documentation of the same flow (2006) noted weak PCDM-DCDM coordination, outdated flood records and poor access to flood information at district level; treat the specifics as dated, the shape as persistent [S16].

## 2. How warnings reach officials versus citizens

EWS1294 is the national system, owned by the government and operated by PCDMs in all 25 provinces; NCDM and the Ministry of Post and Telecommunications sit above it [S4]. Provincial officials compose and broadcast alerts to registered users in their own province from a dashboard usable on a phone or computer; channels are IVR voice calls, loudspeakers (added October 2021) and radio (January 2022) [S1][S4]. As of 2022, 774,400+ alerts had gone to about 120,000 registered users [S3][S4].

Officials are senders on EWS1294, not recipients. They receive hazard information through the MOWRAM email/fax/phone flow, the PCDM chain, and MRC bulletins [S1][S5]. Citizens receive IVR calls, loudspeakers and radio; NPR documented officials relaying warnings through car speakers for people without phones [S8].

Receipt and reach data exist but are thin. All surveyed EWS1294 users had received messages and understood them; 46% preferred IVR and 30% loudspeakers. Among non-users, 79% had never heard of EWS1294, 5% had no phone, and 70% said their village disaster management group had received no capacity training [S3]. A 2011 Ministry of Environment study cited in the same report found 36% of Cambodians got no risk information and 72% of those warned got it too late [S3].

Official coordination groups on Telegram or Viber: none were found in the reviewed sources. NCDM's Telegram handle listed in the 2024 handbook is `ncdmkh` [S1]; on 2026-10-18 its public preview resolved to a personal channel of NCDM's First Vice President with about one subscriber, so it is not a working national channel. Elsewhere, MAFF's extension department uses Facebook, Telegram, YouTube and radio for weather and farming advice [S1], provincial administrations post flood updates on Facebook [S7 photo credit], and the EW4ALL roadmap will create a WFP-NCDM multi-stakeholder coordination platform [S7]. Absence of a documented official flood group is a search result, not proof it does not exist. Inference: coordination more likely runs on phone calls, Facebook and Messenger than on named Telegram groups; the 2025 DataReportal figures report potential ad reach of 12.9M for Facebook and 8.6M for Messenger in Cambodia, with Telegram absent from the country's top-platform list (https://datareportal.com/reports/digital-2025-cambodia).

## 3. How MRC bulletins travel and what agencies do with them

The MRC's Regional Flood and Drought Management Centre in Phnom Penh turns 138 hydro-met stations into forecasts at 22 mainstream points, issuing daily bulletins in the wet season and weekly in the dry season; MRC publishes them on its site and shares them by email with National Mekong Committees, national forecasting agencies, civil society and media, and those agencies then disseminate to national, sub-national and local levels [S5]. Flash flood guidance is separate [S5]. Cambodia's national counterpart is MOWRAM, with the National Flood Forecasting Centre upgraded under ADB's GMS flood and drought project [S1].

What agencies actually do with a bulletin is not documented at commune level in these sources. Inference: NCDM and PCDM officials use MRC and MOWRAM levels to decide whether to compose an EWS1294 alert, because the alert texts recorded by PIN are phrased as river-level and upstream conditions [S3]. Do not state this as fact in the deck.

## 4. What repeats elsewhere, and the evidence behind it

Nepal's BIPAD portal runs six modules (dashboard, incident reporting, damage and loss, real time, risk information, profile) with separate platforms for national, provincial and municipal government, and an impact-based forecasting module built on historical loss data [S10]. The Philippines standardised disaster reporting through DROMIC-IS after finding delays, inconsistent data and inefficient coordination, and the NDRRMP monitoring review identified "absence of standard reporting tools" as a system gap [S11]. Indonesia's PetaBencana.id combines crowd reports with BPBD Jakarta validation and feeds the city's own Pantau Banjir app [S12]. Bangladesh combines trigger-based anticipatory cash with IVR warnings downward and SMS water-level reports upward (Mobile services for flood early warning, Cumiskey et al. 2014, cited in [S3]), and a randomised evaluation found transfers made before the flood peak outperformed equivalent post-flood transfers [S13].

Features that repeat: a standard form or template, one place where reports land, an approval or validation step before data counts as official, an aggregate view by admin unit, tie-in to a hazard forecast, and export for analysis [S1][S10][S11][S12].

Evidence strength differs. The strong quantitative evidence is for anticipatory cash transfers [S13]. For reporting platforms, evidence is institutional (agency statements, programme reviews) rather than measured outcomes: BIPAD is described as supporting risk-informed decisions at all levels [S10], and DROMIC-IS is presented by its own agency as the fix for reporting delays [S11]. Treat platform impact claims as plausible but not established.

## 5. Implications for MekongPulse

Verified codebase facts used for costing: the shared repository already exposes `subscriptions_for_commune(commune_id)` and an `alert_dispatches` table recording commune, risk tier, directive type, bulletin text, dispatch time and recipient count; the frontend is a static Next.js export, so print and file downloads need no server.

| Rank | Feature | Cost in this codebase | Evidence it helps |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Printable commune status summary (print/PDF) | Cheapest. Print CSS plus a summary route over existing risk data. No backend. | Matches the sitrep field set officials already work with [S2]; district-level access to flood information was already flagged as poor in 2006 [S16]; provincial administrations share situation updates as posts and images [S7]. |
| 2 | CSV export of commune risk states | Near free. Client-side blob download or one route handler. No schema change. | PRISM itself is built around export for analysis [S9]; a file is what a district officer forwards upward in the CCDM-PCDM chain [S1]. |
| 3 | Alert event log for officials | Low. The dispatch row already exists at send time; this is a read endpoint plus one page. | Requires no new reporting burden, and gives the only honest record of what was sent, when and to how many, against reach gaps documented for EWS1294 [S3] and the national roadmap's last-mile priority [S7]. |
| 4 | Subscriber coverage per commune | Low to medium. Raw counts are cheap because per-commune subscriptions are already queryable; named lists raise consent, privacy and data-protection questions and need policy, not just code. | Directly addresses the documented registration gap: 79% of non-users had never heard of the system and 5% had no phone [S3]; the roadmap names women, indigenous communities and persons with disabilities as under-reached [S7]. Publish counts, not names. |
| 5 | Damage report capture with photos and export | High. Needs form UI, image storage, moderation, verification, and a decision about who may submit. | Duplicates an existing official pipeline (CCDM/DCDM KoBoToolbox to PRISM, approved at PCDM) [S1]. A parallel record with no approval path is likely to be ignored or to create conflicting numbers. |
| 6 | Two-way acknowledgment from commune officials | Highest. Identity verification, outbound and inbound messaging, anti-spoofing, escalation rules, and a mandate that MekongPulse does not have. | The official alerting channel is province-controlled EWS1294 [S1][S4]. Acknowledgements from a non-government tool carry no authority, and the coordination platform now being built by WFP and NCDM is the likely home for official two-way flows [S7]. |

Ranked order, blunt version: build 1 to 3 now, treat 4 as aggregate counts only, defer 5 and 6 until a government partner asks for them in writing.

Do not build: a competitor to KoBoToolbox/PRISM for damage data [S1][S9]; an alerting portal that duplicates the EWS1294 dashboard [S1][S4]; evacuation or resource-allocation features without a mandate [S2]; a Telegram or Viber "coordination group" replacement, since no official flood group was documented and the coordination platform is already planned [S7]; SMS sending, which is telecommunication-regulated and paid [S1].

Citeable in a competition deck, each traceable to a source above: EWS1294 is national, government-owned, operated by all 25 PCDMs, with about 120,000 registered users and 774,400+ alerts; 100% of surveyed users received and understood alerts; 79% of non-users had never heard of it; 36% of Cambodians got no risk information in the 2011 study; the official damage chain is CCDM to DCDM to PCDM to NCDM with KoBoToolbox and PRISM; the 2022 SitRep numbers; MRC's daily bulletins reach national agencies by email and website; EW4ALL is endorsed but faces a $22.7M gap (about 41% of roadmap cost); 2026 floods affected 255 communes and 73,922 households as of 29 September 2026.

Do not cite: imagined Telegram coordination groups, the EWS1294 message wording (only paraphrases are sourced [S3]), or any claim that MRC bulletins change commune-level decisions, which remains inference.
